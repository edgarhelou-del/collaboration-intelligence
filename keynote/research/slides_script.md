# FULL SLIDE SCRIPT — 35 slides
Format per slide: [#] ACT | ON-SCREEN TEXT | IMAGE DIRECTION | SPEAKER NOTES

## ACT I — THE WORLD CHANGED

[1] TITLE | "Bioadaptability" / subtitle: "What organizations owe the living world, now that AI has changed the rules of execution" | Full-bleed abstract dawn-light gradient over a topographic/contour-line texture (mountains-as-network motif), no logos, no clichés | Cold open. Do not summarize the talk. Walk to center, let the title sit in silence for a beat. Spoken line: "I'm not going to talk to you about biology. Or about AI. I'm going to talk to you about the thing that happens between people, right before either of those matters."
[2] I | "Change used to be an event." | Frozen-motion image: a single dry leaf mid-fall against a static, sharply-focused forest floor — stillness interrupted | Establishing metaphor. Change historically arrived as discrete events organizations could staff a "change management" project around — a merger, a recession, a new regulation. Transition line into next slide.
[3] I | "Now it's the weather." | Same forest, but rendered as continuous motion blur — rain, wind, no single frozen point | Evidence: WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 — AI/information-processing technology is projected to affect 86% of businesses; McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey finds 88% of organizations now use AI regularly in at least one function. Point: adoption is no longer episodic, it's ambient. Spoken line: "You don't schedule a project to adapt to weather. You live inside it, permanently, or you don't."
[4] I | (no text — black frame, then image) | A compass or old instrument for measuring discrete change (sextant, weathervane) shown cracked or clearly antique/obsolete, resting on a modern surface | Transition into Act II. The tools organizations built to manage change as an event are still the tools most of them are using. Spoken line: "Most of our organizations are still built for a world with punctuation. Full stops. Beginnings and endings. We're going to talk about what happens when the punctuation disappears."

## ACT II — THE EXECUTION PARADOX

[5] II | "Idea → Prototype: 6 weeks → 6 minutes" (rendered as a literal collapsing bracket/timeline graphic, not a corporate chart) | Split frame: left = hand-sketched notebook page; right = the same sketch as a rendered, working interface, the negative space between them physically compressed | Concrete evidence, minimal chrome. Cite: randomized field experiment (Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon, Demirer, 2023) found developers with GitHub Copilot completed a defined coding task 55.8% faster than a control group. Cite: Dell'Acqua/Mollick/BCG-HBS field experiment (2023-25) — inside AI's competence, consultants using GPT-4 were >25% faster and >40% higher quality. Spoken line: "This is not a marketing claim. This is a randomized trial."
[6] II | "One person. Six months. $80M." | A single chair at a plain desk, one laptop, harsh directional light — deliberately unglamorous, opposite of "startup hustle" stock imagery | Story: Maor Shlomo built Base44 (an AI app-building tool) essentially alone, hit $1M ARR three weeks after launch, and sold the six-month-old company to Wix for $80M cash — without ever hiring a team or raising venture funding. Point: this is what happens when the distance between an idea and a shipped product collapses to nearly zero for one person. Transition: if one person can do this, what happens inside an organization of thousands of these people?
[7] II | PARADOX 1: "The cheaper it gets to execute an idea, the more expensive a bad one becomes." | Single word "expensive" in stark type over a plain dark field — no image, let the line breathe | This is paradox #1, stated directly to the room. Do not explain it immediately — let it sit. Then: when execution was slow, a bad idea died quietly in a six-month delay. Now it ships by Friday, in front of customers, before anyone has had time to be sure it was worth building.
[8] II | "If everyone can execute — what becomes scarce?" | The question alone, white text, full black frame | The pivot question of the entire talk. Say it slowly. Let it sit for 3-4 full seconds of silence before advancing. This is the hinge the rest of the talk turns on.
[9] II | (single word, revealed after a beat) "Judgment." | A single narrow shaft of light in an otherwise dark, empty architectural space (a stairwell, a grain silo interior) — scale and emptiness, not a lightbulb cliché | Transition into Act III. Not "creativity," not "talent" in the abstract — specifically the capacity to judge, together, which of the infinite things that can now be built is actually worth building. Spoken line: "Not creativity. Not talent. Judgment — and specifically, judgment that more than one person has agreed to."

## ACT III — THE NEW BOTTLENECK

[10] III | "Sensemaking is not a soft skill. It's the whole game now." | Blueprint/architectural drawing of a bridge under construction, mid-build, workers visible only as small silhouettes against the structure's scale | Define sensemaking without jargon: the ongoing, social process of noticing a change, building a shared story about what it means, and committing to act on that story before it's too late to matter. It has always existed in organizations. It was never the constraint before, because execution was slower than sensemaking. Now sensemaking is often the slowest step in the pipeline.
[11] III | "$250M lost to indecision. Every year. One Fortune 500 company." | Empty conference table, harsh fluorescent overhead light, chairs pushed in, nobody present | Evidence: McKinsey's global decision-making survey (2019) found only ~20% of organizations excel at decision-making; 61% of respondents say at least half their decision-making time is used ineffectively; a typical Fortune 500 company loses roughly 530,000 manager-days a year — about $250M — to bad decision processes. Point: this was already true before generative AI. AI just removed every other excuse for it.
[12] III | PARADOX 2: "We didn't build slow organizations by accident. Slowness was what caught bad judgment before it shipped. AI didn't fix the flaw in that mechanism. It deleted the mechanism." | A large mechanical gear or industrial governor (the literal device that regulates speed in old machinery), photographed close, isolated, motionless | This paradox reframes bureaucratic slowness — usually mocked — as having done real, if clumsy, work: it was a rate-limiter on error. The talk is not nostalgic for bureaucracy. It's naming what has to be replaced, not just removed.
[13] III | "This isn't a new problem. Life has been solving it for 3.8 billion years." | A single fossilized stromatolite or ancient rock strata formation, cross-section, geological time made visible in layers | Bridge into Act IV. Organizations are roughly 200 years old as a designed institution. Distributed sensing and collective decision-making under uncertainty is a problem biology has been iterating on since the origin of life. We're not the first system to face this. Spoken line: "So before we design anything new, it's worth looking at what already works."

## ACT IV — LOOK AT LIFE

[14] IV | "None of what you're about to see is a metaphor for teamwork. It's the mechanism." | Extreme macro shot: a single hyphal thread of fungal mycelium in sharp focus, forest litter blurred behind it | Framing slide before the nature sequence. Explicitly tell the audience: this is not a biology lecture, and it's not a cute analogy. Each system below runs on a specific, documented mechanism, and that mechanism has a direct engineering equivalent in how information moves through your company.
[15] IV | "Mycelium: intelligence in the network, never in one node." | Wide, high-contrast image of an exposed root/fungal network system, roots reading almost as a nervous system | Evidence: Suzanne Simard's 1997 Nature study (the paper that coined "the wood-wide web") showed carbon moving between different tree species through shared mycorrhizal fungal networks; her ongoing Mother Tree research shows large, well-connected "hub" trees disproportionately buffer and redistribute resources to stressed seedlings, and removing hub trees measurably harms the whole stand's regeneration. Org translation: invest in your organization's "hub" people and teams — the ones many others quietly route information and trust through — because the network's resilience depends on them disproportionately, and no one put them on an org chart.
[16] IV | "Bees don't vote for the loudest bee. They wait for a quorum." | Tight frame on a swarm cluster, texture of hundreds of individual bodies reading as one mass | Evidence: Thomas Seeley's Honeybee Democracy (2010) — hundreds of scout bees independently evaluate candidate nest sites; better sites get longer, more vigorous waggle dances, recruiting more scouts; the swarm commits only once roughly 15-20 scouts are simultaneously present at one site — a quorum, not a majority vote or a single strong advocate. Org translation: a good decision process doesn't ask "who argued hardest," it asks "how many independent people, who actually went and checked, arrived at the same place."
[17] IV | "No bird sees the shape. The shape is what's left when nobody's in charge." | Murmuration cropped tight enough that it reads first as pure abstract geometry, not "a flock of birds" | Evidence: Iain Couzin's research (Nature, 2005) shows a small informed minority — sometimes under 10% — can steer a large group accurately, because local copying amplifies the signal; his 2011 Science paper found the counterintuitive twist that adding uninformed, non-partisan individuals to a group split between two competing factions restores majority consensus and prevents a vocal minority from capturing the decision. Org translation: your quietest, least-opinionated people in a meeting may be doing more to protect the quality of the decision than your most articulate one.
[18] IV | "Your immune system never asks permission to notice something is wrong." | Macro cell-scale image, immune cells engaging a foreign body, clinical but striking | Evidence: innate immunity gives every cell cheap, fast, generic pattern-recognition (detect first, ask questions later); adaptive immunity then generates enormous diversity and clonally amplifies whatever specifically matches the threat — detect broadly and cheaply everywhere, then scale a precise response fast once something real is confirmed, and keep a memory of it afterward so the next response is faster. Org translation: don't centralize the right to notice something is wrong. Centralize, instead, how fast a confirmed signal gets resourced.
[19] IV | "A forest that never burns isn't healthy. It's overdue." — Turn: "None of these systems is smarter than the people in this room. Their coordination is." | Aftermath image: green shoots emerging through blackened, burned forest floor — regeneration, not devastation, as the dominant read | Evidence: C.S. Holling's resilience research (1973) and the "adaptive cycle" — systems that over-optimize for efficient, tightly-connected growth (the "K" phase) become brittle; periodic disturbance and reorganization, fueled by diversity banked during the stable phase, is what keeps the system adaptive long-term. Org translation: an organization with zero failed projects, zero sunset products, zero internal disagreement isn't healthy — it's accumulating brittleness it hasn't paid for yet. Then deliver the turn line as the act's closing thesis, and pause before Act V.

## ACT V — THE COLLECTIVE BRAIN

[20] V | "A group can be smarter than its smartest member. The evidence is real — and it's contested." | Chalk/whiteboard-style abstract of overlapping circles (a Venn-like form, hand-drawn, not corporate clip-art) with visible erasure marks — showing revision, not certainty | Evidence, presented honestly: Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi & Malone (Science, 2010) found a statistical "collective intelligence factor" predicting group performance across many tasks, correlated with average social sensitivity and equality of conversational turn-taking — barely correlated with the group's average or maximum individual IQ. But: Credé & Howardson (2017) published a direct methodological challenge arguing the data don't support one general factor; the debate is still live in the literature (2021 meta-analyses found mixed replication). Say this plainly: the audience should not leave thinking this is settled science — they should leave knowing it's a real, still-open argument, which is more credible than a clean story.
[21] V | PARADOX 3: "The smartest person in the room is not always the smartest part of the room." | Empty chair at the head of a long table, all other chairs occupied but out of focus | Evidence: Irving Janis's Groupthink (1972) — cohesive, high-consensus groups (his cases: Bay of Pigs, Pearl Harbor) systematically suppressed dissent and made worse decisions than individuals would have alone; Steiner (1972) formalized "process loss" — actual group output = potential output minus real coordination and motivation losses. This is the "too many cooks" problem, and it's measured, not folklore.
[22] V | "Diversity doesn't automatically make a group smarter. It makes the wrong question louder." | Two adjacent close-up textures — smooth river stones vs. jagged broken rock — same material, radically different result of the same forces | Evidence, deliberately complicating the earlier praise of diversity: Scott Page's "diversity trumps ability" finding (The Difference, 2007) holds specifically for hard problems with no single dominant solver, and only with good communication and shared goals — his own later work ("Diversity Paradoxes") maps when it fails. Jehn, Northcraft & Neale's "faultlines" research shows that when diversity splits a group along correlated demographic lines rather than cutting across them, it increases conflict instead of insight. Point: "more different people" is not the lever. "The right cross-cutting differences, with real communication," is.
[23] V | "Three engineers rebuilt a supply chain from a fax machine and a rumor. No one told them to." | Documentary-style image: a hand-drawn technical sketch/blueprint pinned to a factory wall, faxed-paper texture visible | Story: the 1997 Aisin fire destroyed Toyota's sole source of a critical brake valve, wiping out 6,000 molds. Over 200 independent supplier firms self-organized without central coordination — some with no prior experience making the part — reverse-engineering tooling from faxed blueprints, competing informally to be first with a working part. Toyota resumed production in two days; full recovery took about five, against outside predictions of weeks. Point: nobody designed this recovery. It emerged from a dense, trusted network acting on local information in parallel — this is a real-world mycelium network, in an auto-parts supply chain.
[24] V | "Psychological safety isn't a culture poster. It's the wiring that lets the network route around damage." | A single unbroken thread stitched through torn fabric, visible mend | Evidence: Amy Edmondson's 1999 field study of 51 teams found psychological safety — not team confidence or efficacy — predicted learning behavior, which in turn predicted performance. Google's internal Project Aristotle study (2015, 180+ teams) independently found psychological safety mattered more than who was on the team. Org translation: it's not a soft perk. It's the precondition for the group's information to actually circulate instead of being quietly withheld.
[25] V | "Now put a machine in the room." | A single empty chair, ordinary, unremarkable, at a round table — no robot, no glow, just an empty seat that reads as deliberately reserved | Bridge into Act VI. Everything just discussed — quorum, diversity, psychological safety, process loss — describes how humans think together. What happens when one of the participants in that room is not human?

## ACT VI — AI ENTERS THE SYSTEM

[26] VI | "700 employees' worth of work. One assistant. One month." | Clean, minimal customer-service headset resting on an empty desk, unused, slightly out of focus | Story: Klarna's OpenAI-built customer service assistant handled 2.3 million chats in its first month in 2024 — the equivalent workload of 700 full-time agents — cutting average resolution time from 11 minutes to 2. This is real, it is not hype, and it happened in weeks, not years.
[27] VI | PARADOX 4: "Klarna's CEO later admitted they'd 'cut too far, too fast' — and rehired the humans." | Same headset image, now with a hand reaching to pick it back up | The correction: by 2025, Klarna's CEO publicly said quality suffered and reintroduced human agents. This is the cleanest live example available of the entire talk's central claim: execution (handling the chat volume) was never the problem. Judgment (how far to push the substitution, and when) was — and that judgment failed at the individual-decision level, not the AI's execution level.
[28] VI | "AI makes each of you more creative. It can still make all of you sound the same." | A single figure's silhouette, then the same silhouette repeated in a row, identical, fading toward the edge of frame | Evidence, deliberate contradiction of techno-optimism: Bogert, Schecter & Watson (Science Advances, 2024) found writers using generative AI produced individually more creative, better-reviewed stories — but the resulting body of stories was measurably more similar to each other. Individually novel, collectively homogeneous. Point: cheaper individual execution does not automatically add up to a smarter collective. Left unmanaged, it can quietly narrow it.
[29] VI | "The question was never how smart your people are with AI. It's how smart is what they build together." | A single wide shot: many small individual desk lamps switched on across an otherwise dark open office floor, each pool of light separate, none of them touching | Reframe line closing the act. Every individual in the organization now has access to something close to a research team, a design studio, and an engineering department, on demand. The org chart hasn't caught up to what that means. Transition into Act VII.

## ACT VII — FROM INDIVIDUAL TO SYSTEM INTELLIGENCE

[30] VII | "Amateurs solved a protein structure professionals couldn't crack for over a decade — in ten days." | Screen-capture-style abstract of interlocking, puzzle-like organic shapes (a protein-fold silhouette, rendered as pure form, not literal science-diagram) | Story: the Foldit Contenders Group — players of an online protein-folding game, most with no biochemistry training — solved the crystal structure of a retroviral protease that had stumped professional structural biologists for over a decade, within about ten days; they're credited as co-authors on the resulting peer-reviewed Nature Structural & Molecular Biology paper. Point: this is not a story about AI. It's a reminder that "the system is smarter than any individual in it" is not a new idea AI introduced — AI is what happens when you add a new kind of participant to a very old game.
[31] VII | PARADOX 5: "We built AI to make individuals more powerful. The organizations that win will use it to make the group more honest." | Two overlapping circles of light on a dark floor, one much larger and brighter than the other, the smaller one being deliberately repositioned to overlap more | This is the last paradox and the clearest statement of the thesis's mechanism: individual capability and collective intelligence are not the same variable, and optimizing hard for one does not guarantee gains in the other — sometimes, per the homogenization evidence, it trades against it. The deliberate design choice is what closes that gap.

## ACT VIII — THE ADAPTIVE ORGANIZATION

[32] VIII | SENSE → INTERPRET → CONNECT → EXPERIMENT → ACT → LEARN → ADAPT | The cycle rendered as a natural spiral (nautilus cross-section, or a tree-ring cross-section) rather than a corporate flowchart — literally grown, not drawn | Present this as the logical conclusion of everything already shown — the bee swarm's scout-dance-quorum sequence, the immune system's detect-amplify-remember sequence, and Toyota's andon-cord sequence are all instances of the same underlying loop. Don't introduce it as a new framework; introduce it as "here's the pattern you've already seen five times tonight, named."
[33] VIII | "Any worker on the line can stop a multibillion-dollar factory. On purpose." | A single pull-cord or rope hanging in an industrial space, isolated, backlit | Evidence: Toyota's andon cord — any assembly-line worker, not a supervisor, can halt the line the instant they sense a problem (Jidoka, "automation with a human touch"). This is a company that deliberately re-engineered where in the hierarchy the authority to say "something is wrong" lives — pushing it to whoever is standing closest to the signal, not whoever has the highest title. Org translation: ask, honestly, who in your organization currently has "andon cord" authority — and whether it's the person closest to the problem.
[34] VIII | "Not every network survives contact with scale. Say so." | A frayed rope, cleanly cut and re-spliced, the repair visible and deliberately not hidden | Necessary counter-example, for intellectual honesty: the "Spotify Model" of autonomous squads was published in 2012 as a snapshot, not a prescription — and was copied by thousands of companies anyway. In 2020, Spotify's own alumni documented in detail that the company had quietly abandoned it: autonomy without real coordination mechanisms produced duplicated technology stacks and unresolved power struggles. The lesson isn't "decentralize." It's "decentralize with the same seriousness you'd bring to any other piece of architecture — and be willing to say when it's not working."

## ACT IX — THE HUMAN ADVANTAGE / CLOSE

[35] IX | "Human intelligence. Times artificial intelligence. Times collective intelligence. The winners will be the product, not any one term." Final line, alone, then image, then nothing: "Perhaps the advantage was never in the smartest person in the building, or the most powerful model running on its servers. Perhaps it was always in how fast a group of people — and now, the systems they build with — can turn a weak signal into a shared decision. That was always the real work. AI just made it impossible to keep pretending it wasn't." | A single tree standing in an open field at first light, alone but clearly rooted in unseen ground — the mycelium network from slide 15 implied, not shown | Closing. Deliver the final line slowly. Let the image hold. End in silence — no "thank you" slide, no bullet-point recap, no logo. Let the room sit with it for a beat before applause.
